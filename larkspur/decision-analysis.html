One co-packer, one hurricane coast, and a decision due this quarter

The vulnerability assessment ranked the Gonzales aseptic co-packer first among Larkspur's dependencies. This page takes that one dependency through a decision analysis: what has to be decided, which physical conditions are worth testing, what they would do to operations and the accounts, and which response holds up. It is built in steps, and each section appears when its step is done.

Step 1 · Decision and boundary

The decision sets what the scenarios have to answer

Should Larkspur commit this quarter to reducing its dependence on the single aseptic co-packer at Gonzales, Louisiana, before the 2027 hurricane season, and by which means?

Who decides

Marcus Webb (COO) and Priya Natarajan (CFO), with the President of Beverage; the Treasurer and General Counsel for the insurance alternative

Decide by

Mid-November 2026 if a second site is to be saleable before the 2027 season peak on the slower (nine-month) qualification

Horizon

Hurricane seasons 2027 to 2029, the remaining term of the co-manufacturing agreement. A mid-century view is used only to ask whether the hazard is getting worse.

A0

Hold the current position

27 days of Beverage finished goods, about 14 of them staged at the co-packer; contingent business interruption cover of $10.0M after a 30-day wait.

A1

Move and build the buffer

Ship finished goods out of Gonzales faster and build extra cover ahead of August to October. Limited by line capacity: Lines 2 and 3 already run at about 95% of rated capacity.

A2

Qualify a second aseptic site

Process-authority validation, regulatory filings and line trials at another facility. The last line took 6 to 9 months. Cost is not in the client records.

A3

Change the insurance

Raise the contingent business interruption limit or shorten its waiting period at renewal. Pays money; does not keep product on the shelf.

How the alternatives will be compared

  • Contribution lost beyond the buffer, $M
  • Months with retail case fill under 98.5%, 97% and 96% (credits at 3% and 5%; category review after three months under 96%)
  • Effect on adjusted EBITDA against the $59.4M of headroom under the 3.50x covenant at the October test
  • What stays uninsured after the $10.0M contingent limit
  • Cost of the action and how long before it protects anything

Boundary

  • In: River Parish Aseptic Packaging Lines 2 and 3; the four product lines filled there; finished goods staged on site and at the Romeoville distribution centre; the electricity, water and road access the site needs; retail service-level terms.
  • Out: San Antonio bottling and the Holland, Michigan co-packer; pea-protein supply from Fargo into the functional line (8% of Fargo output), noted for a later run.

Hurricane Ida, August 2021

The centre passed 21 km from the site still at hurricane strength. Nearly every customer in the parish lost power. More than 100 roads closed, including Interstate 10 between Prairieville and Gramercy. Entergy's published restoration estimate for Ascension was 7 September, day 9.

What it would test. How long the lines stay down against the 13- and 27-day buffer marks.

Record 1 · 2 · 3 · 4

August 2016 flood

Not a hurricane and outside any storm forecast. 459 mm of rain in four days at Baton Rouge. The Amite River peaked at 266,000 cubic feet a second, 2.4 times the previous record in a series back to 1921. About 16,000 homes and businesses in the parish were damaged, and flooding went far beyond the mapped 1% flood plain. Power was a small part of it.

What it would test. Water in the plant: staged stock, Larkspur-owned filler tooling and a restart measured in months.

Hurricane Gustav, then Ike, September 2008

Centre 66 km away at hurricane strength. A 91 mph gust at Baton Rouge, the strongest there since Betsy in 1965. Between 75% and 100% of customers were out from the coast through Baton Rouge, and Entergy counted 964,000 customers affected. Ike brought tropical-storm conditions to the parish ten days later. What it would test. A second storm arriving before the first is repaired.

Record 1 · 2 · 3

Evidence that a storm could outrun the buffer

  • Ida kept more than half the parish without power for a week and about a fifth for 17 days. That is past the 13-day mark that applies if the stock staged at the plant is lost or cannot be reached.
  • The buffer clock counts days without production, not days without power. Restarting an aseptic line after a long outage, getting staff back and getting trucks through closed roads all add days the outage record does not show.
  • The 2016 flood was 2.4 times the previous river record and went well beyond the mapped flood plain. A site that the flood map calls protected is not shown to be safe from a repeat.
  • Storms come in runs. Ike followed Gustav by ten days in 2008 and Zeta followed Delta by 19 days in 2020.
  • After any stop, the lines have about 5.5% spare capacity to rebuild the buffer.

Evidence against

  • In the outage record from 2014 to 2025, every event except Ida was down to under a tenth of customers within five days. Ida itself cleared by day 18, inside the 27-day buffer.
  • Twelve storms passed within 150 km at tropical-storm strength or more in the 21 seasons from 2005 to 2025. One, possibly two with Gustav, produced an outage measured in weeks.
  • The register coordinates sit in FEMA Zone X, an area of reduced flood risk behind a levee, about 10 feet above sea level. Hurricane surge has reached only the far east of the parish near Lake Maurepas.
  • Utilities restore critical and large customers first. The plant may come back sooner than the parish median, and it may have its own generation.
  • Nothing in Larkspur's records mentions a stoppage at the co-packer in any of these events. The records hold no operating history before 2025, so they cannot rule one out either; the co-packer would know.

A reading that needs care. From day 8 to day 17 after Ida the count sits near 11,000 customers and then falls to about 100 within hours. That may be a real block of customers, or one utility's feed that stopped updating. The 18-day figure is therefore an upper reading; Entergy's own estimate for the parish was day 9.

Step 3 · Scenario set · proposed

Four cases, each chosen for what it shows about the decision

None of these carries a probability. Each is a physically documented event, or a stated stress on one, followed through to what it would mean at the plant. Ranges marked as assumptions are ours and are the first things to confirm with the co-packer.

S1

Ida again

Historical replay · The relevant event

Shows where interruption begins under the current position and how long recovery takes. It is the worst storm in the outage record, and its range straddles the day-13 mark and comes close to day 27.

Physical conditions

  • A hurricane makes landfall in late August and its centre passes about 20 km from the plant, still at hurricane strength. Record source
  • Nearly every customer in the parish loses power. Half are back by day 7; the parish estimate is day 9; the outage record clears by day 18. Record source
  • More than 100 roads close, including Interstate 10, for the first days. Little rain: this is a wind event. Record source

What it means at the plant

  • The plant is without grid power for 9 to 18 days. AssumptionParish range; the plant's own feeder and any backup generation are unknown.
  • Restart takes a further 3 to 7 days: re-sterilising the aseptic lines, staff returning, inbound cartons and ingredients. AssumptionOurs; the co-packer's plant manager would know.
  • So Lines 2 and 3 stop for 12 to 25 days. Staged stock survives but cannot ship for the first 3 to 5 days. CalculatedSum of the two ranges above.

What it lets us say. What the current buffer does in a repeat of the worst storm on record. Not how likely that storm is.

S2

Water in the plant

Severity and duration · The feature that breaks every buffer

Tests the one condition that takes out the staged stock and the lines together. It is where the contingent cover runs out and where a second site or more insurance would show their value.

Physical conditions

  • About 460 mm of rain falls in four days, with no tropical storm and little warning. Record source
  • The Amite River peaks at 2.4 times its previous record; flooding reaches well beyond the mapped 1% flood plain and damages about 16,000 homes and businesses in the parish. Record source
  • Water enters the co-packer's plant. AssumptionA stress assumption. The flood map shows the register coordinates as protected by a levee, and nothing records water at this site in 2016.

What it means at the plant

  • The 14 days of finished goods staged on site are lost, and Larkspur-owned filler tooling is damaged. AssumptionFollows from water in the plant; the tooling is on a bailment schedule in the agreement.
  • Lines 2 and 3 stop for 60 to 150 days. AssumptionLower end is a repair-and-revalidate estimate; upper end is the agreement's 150-day termination trigger. The last new line took 6 to 9 months, so longer is possible.

What it lets us say. What a flood at the site would cost and which alternative changes that. Not that the site will flood.

S3

A second storm before recovery

Compound or sequential · Recovery under pressure

Tests whether the buffer can be rebuilt in time. With about 5.5% spare capacity, the lines are still short when the next storm arrives.

Physical conditions

  • A storm like S1, then a second one 10 to 19 days after the first landfall. Record sourceIke followed Gustav by ten days in 2008; Zeta followed Delta by 19 days in 2020.
  • The second storm is of the Francine kind: most of the parish out at the peak, under a tenth by day 2. Record source

What it means at the plant

  • A second stop of 2 to 5 days, arriving while the buffer is empty or nearly so and repairs from the first storm are unfinished. AssumptionThe link between the two is operational: depleted stock and incomplete repairs. We do not claim the first storm makes the second more likely.

What it lets us say. Whether a larger or better-placed buffer helps when the lines cannot refill it. Not a forecast of clustered seasons.

S4

Francine

Historical replay · The useful contrast

The storm the business rides through. It shows the current position working, gives the model a case where the answer should be close to zero, and stands for most of the twelve storms since 2005.

Physical conditions

  • Centre passes 36 km away at tropical-storm strength. 70% of parish customers lose power; under a tenth by day 2. Record source

What it means at the plant

  • Lines 2 and 3 stop for 2 to 4 days, well inside the buffer. AssumptionOutage plus a short restart.

What it lets us say. Whether doing nothing is enough for the common storm. A large loss here would point to an error in the model before anything else.

Step 4 · Enterprise consequences · first run

The buffer absorbs the storms. A flood at the plant costs $5M to $35M and puts the retail listings under review

Results for the current position: 27 days of cover, 14 of them staged at the plant, and $10.0M of contingent cover after 30 days. Each range runs from the short end of the case to the long end. Run R-20261001-02; every figure below is Calculated from that run unless marked.

  • The buffer absorbs the storms. With 27 days of cover, a repeat of Ida stops the lines for 12 to 25 days and no sale is lost. Francine costs nothing. A second storm on top of a long stop loses three days of sales.
  • The cost of a storm is deferred, not avoided. The lines have about 5.5% spare capacity, so a 25-day stop takes until day 474 to rebuild. The 2028 season would open with 16 days of cover instead of 27.
  • The flood is the case that costs money: $5M to $35M of EBITDA after insurance. The contingent cover pays at most $10M and nothing in the first 30 days.
  • No case breaches the 3.50x covenant on its own. The worst reaches 3.18x, or 3.31x if the cash lost is added to net debt.
  • The largest exposure is not in those figures. In the flood case retail fill stays under 96% for three to six months, which opens a category review at all three retailers. Losing the Gonzales lines at Lakemont alone for a year is $26.7M of contribution; at all three, $47.5M. Added to the flood case, that passes the $59.4M of covenant headroom.
  • Service credits work like a cliff. Two days without stock in a month put category fill under 96%, and from a day and a half the 5% credit applies to the month's Beverage invoices, about $1.1M.

How it was calculated

A daily balance of finished goods, counted in days of demand: stock plus production, less shipments, with production capped at the lines' rated capacity. Unmet demand is treated as a lost sale and priced at the lines' contribution margin. Service credits follow the retailer terms by calendar month. Contingent cover pays lost contribution after its 30-day wait, up to its limit. The low end of each range uses the shorter stop, credits on Gonzales-line invoices only, and assumes the lost stock is recovered from others; the high end uses the longer stop, credits on all Beverage invoices, and the stock loss borne in full.

Not priced: expediting freight, standby-line charges, damage to Larkspur-owned tooling, launch costs, loss of a retail listing, tax. Demand is taken as flat through the year, and each retailer's Beverage purchases as 60% Gonzales lines.

Calculated Key figures were recomputed from closed-form arithmetic in a separate script (check_scenarios.py), using figures typed from the client records. All 31 comparisons agree; the largest difference is one day in the rebuild date, a counting convention.
